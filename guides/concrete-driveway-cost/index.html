How Much Does a Concrete Driveway Cost in 2026?

June 2026 10 min read Concrete

A concrete driveway is one of the most common flatwork projects in residential construction — and one of the most frequently misquoted. Whether you're a homeowner getting bids or a contractor pricing the job, understanding the real cost breakdown helps you make smarter decisions and avoid surprises.

Average Concrete Driveway Cost in 2026

For a standard brushed-finish concrete driveway, expect to pay between $8 and $18 per square foot installed, depending on your location, thickness, site conditions, and finish. A typical two-car driveway (600 sq ft) runs $4,800 to $10,800 all-in.

Cost Factors That Affect Your Price

1. Size and Thickness

The biggest cost driver is total square footage and pour thickness. Residential driveways are typically poured at 4 inches minimum, with 5–6 inches recommended for areas with heavy vehicles (trucks, RVs). Each additional inch of thickness adds roughly $0.75–$1.25 per sq ft in concrete material cost.

2. Finish Type

A basic broom/brushed finish is included in the standard price. Decorative finishes add significant cost:

3. Site Access and Grading

If the concrete truck can't back within chute distance (~12 feet) of the pour site, you'll need a concrete pump at $150–$300/hour (plus a $200–$400 minimum). Significant grading or soil removal to prepare a proper subbase adds $1–$3/sq ft.

4. Subbase Preparation

A proper subbase is non-negotiable for driveway longevity. You'll typically need 4–6 inches of compacted gravel or crushed stone beneath the slab. If the existing soil is expansive clay or organic material, it may need to be excavated and replaced, adding $2–$5/sq ft.

5. Regional Variation

Concrete prices vary significantly by region. Ready-mix concrete runs $130–$160/yd³ in the Midwest and Southeast, but $160–$220/yd³ in the Northeast, West Coast, and Hawaii. Labor rates follow similar patterns.

Material vs Labor Cost Breakdown

For a standard brushed-finish driveway, the cost typically splits roughly:

  • Concrete material: 25–30% of total cost ($2.00–$5.00/sq ft)
  • Labor (forming, pouring, finishing): 45–55% ($3.50–$8.00/sq ft)
  • Subbase, grading, formwork materials: 15–20% ($1.00–$3.00/sq ft)
  • Reinforcement (rebar/mesh/fiber): 5–10% ($0.50–$1.50/sq ft)

Rebar or welded wire mesh is strongly recommended for driveways. Fiber mesh alone does not replace structural reinforcement — it reduces surface cracking but won't prevent slab failure under load. Budget for #4 rebar at 18" on center or 6×6 W2.9/W2.9 welded wire mesh.

Decorative Concrete Options

Stamped Concrete

Stamped concrete uses rubber mats pressed into wet concrete to create patterns that mimic natural stone, brick, or slate. Total installed cost runs $12–$25/sq ft. The higher cost comes from the specialized labor, release agents, and sealers required. Stamped concrete requires resealing every 2–3 years ($0.50–$1.50/sq ft per application).

Exposed Aggregate

The top layer of cement paste is washed away after partial curing to reveal the decorative stone aggregate beneath. It costs $10–$20/sq ft installed and creates an excellent non-slip surface. The aggregate stone mix must be specified at the time of ordering — you can't add it after the fact.

Stained Concrete

Acid staining or water-based staining can be applied to new or existing concrete. Integral color (mixed into the concrete) runs $0.75–$2.00/sq ft and provides consistent color throughout. Topical acid stain runs $2–$5/sq ft and creates a more variegated, natural appearance.

Cost-Saving Tips

  1. Get 3+ bids: Prices vary 20–40% between contractors in the same area. Always compare apples to apples — same thickness, finish, and subbase spec.
  2. Time it right: Late fall and winter are typically slower seasons for concrete work. You may get 10–15% lower bids outside of peak season (spring/summer).
  3. Simplify the shape: Curves, angles, and multi-level pours add forming complexity and labor time. A straight rectangular driveway is the most cost-effective shape.
  4. Keep the brushed finish: A standard broom finish is durable, slip-resistant, and significantly cheaper than decorative options.
  5. Prep the site yourself: If you can handle demolition and removal of an old driveway and have the grading done before the crew arrives, you can save $1–$3/sq ft in site prep costs.
  6. Order concrete efficiently: A partial yard costs as much as a full yard in many markets due to short-load fees. Work with your contractor to batch pours if you have multiple small projects.

DIY vs Hiring a Contractor

Concrete work is not a typical DIY project. Here's why:

  • Concrete is time-sensitive — once the truck arrives, you have 60–90 minutes before it starts to set
  • Proper finishing (screeding, bull-floating, edging, jointing, broom finishing) requires skill and timing
  • A failed pour means breaking out the entire slab and starting over — at double the cost
  • Formwork must be level, properly staked, and set to the correct elevation for drainage

When DIY makes sense: Small pads under 50 sq ft (using bagged concrete), walkway sections, or repairs to existing slabs. For anything driveway-scale, hire a qualified concrete contractor.

Always verify your contractor carries general liability insurance and workers' comp. Ask for photos of recent driveway pours and at least two references from jobs completed in the last 12 months.

How Long Does a Concrete Driveway Last?

A properly installed concrete driveway with adequate thickness, proper subbase, control joints, and good drainage will last 25–50 years. Key factors that shorten lifespan include:

  • Inadequate subbase compaction (causes settling and cracking)
  • Missing or improperly spaced control joints (cracks will find their own path)
  • Deicing salt use (accelerates surface spalling in freeze-thaw climates)
  • Tree roots growing beneath the slab
  • Poor drainage causing water to pool on or under the slab
